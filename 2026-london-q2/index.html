Russell Miles

Russell Miles

ClearBank

The waterfall debate isn’t dead. It just changed shape. When AI entered software engineering, it brought with it the oldest metaphor in the industry: the factory. Developers prompt. AI produces. Code ships. Faster, cheaper, more. The conveyor belt is back, and this time it’s powered by something that sounds like it understands you.

But the factory metaphor was wrong in 1970, and it is wrong now. Software engineering was never manufacturing. It was never an assembly line. Peter Naur told us in 1985 that programming is theory building. A fundamentally cognitive, creative, collaborative act. The code was never the whole value. The understanding was.

This keynote takes a deliberately contentious proposition and uses it to crack open the metaphors that still govern how teams think about software, collaboration, and intelligence. Drawing from the AI Literacy for Software Engineers framework, cognitive science, philosophy of mind, and 500 years of technology panics, you’ll take away practical tools to see your own metaphors, challenge them, and begin designing collaboration spaces where human and artificial intelligence actually thrive together.

You will leave with a new way to see your relationship with AI tools. Not as a faster you. Not as a threat. As a fundamentally different kind of intelligence that requires a fundamentally different kind of collaboration space: From factory-thinking to habitat thinking.

Kate Tertiienko

Kate Tertiienko

Infonetica

AI-assisted development is quickly becoming an expectation rather than a choice. Under constant pressure to deliver faster, engineers increasingly delegate not just implementation, but exploration, learning, and design thinking to AI tools. While this can create short-term productivity gains, it also introduces quieter risks: declining system understanding, reduced curiosity, and an explosion of AI-generated code and documentation that shifts cognitive load onto reviewers and senior engineers. Drawing on two decades of experience building and scaling systems across multiple countries, startups, and regulated domains, the speaker examines how this dynamic affects engineers at different stages of their careers. Experienced engineers may still recognise when to question or override AI-generated solutions, but those entering the industry risk shipping systems they have never fully reasoned about. Rather than arguing for restricting AI usage, this talk focuses on the harder problem: how to build an engineering culture where judgment, ownership, and understanding remain first-class expectations, even when AI is everywhere. Attendees will leave with practical cultural and leadership patterns for ensuring AI amplifies engineering capability instead of eroding it.

20 years of hands-on engineering experience building and scaling real production systems across startups and regulated industries. Led and reviewed systems under sustained delivery pressure, with long-term ownership of code quality, reliability, and maintainability. Scaled engineering teams in resource-constrained environments, balancing speed with sustainable engineering practices. First-hand experience using multiple AI development tools in production, with a clear view of their impact on learning, judgment, and system understanding.

Adefisayo Adejumo

Adefisayo Adejumo

Full Stack Engineer

Early in my backend career, I worked alone on systems handling real money and real users. I locked my laptop before leaving my desk, not because a policy required it, but because an unattended terminal with database access is an unguarded door. Then we started deploying LLM agents, and nobody locked anything. Today, many agents operate as permanent unattended sessions with production credentials. They call APIs, read data, and execute actions inside real systems without scoped identity, attribution, or runtime approval. In practice, an LLM agent connected to tools is an unattended session with system access. The engineers wiring agents to production systems are making security decisions by default. This talk makes those decisions deliberate. It introduces the LAR framework: Lock, Attribute, Require — three practical backend patterns for governing LLM agents at runtime. Lock. Agents should not hold standing credentials. Instead, systems generate dynamic, task-scoped credentials that exist only for the duration of an agent session. This reduces the blast radius of a compromised agent and applies the same principle engineers follow when locking their terminals. Attribute. Every agent action must be traceable. Using OAuth identity federation and delegated scopes, tool calls carry identity context so that logs clearly show which user initiated the request, which agent executed it, and what permissions were granted at that moment. Require. Certain actions should never be executed automatically. Destructive changes, financial transactions, or sensitive operations should pause until a human explicitly authorises them. Using approval flows such as Client-Initiated Backchannel Authentication, agents can request permission before completing irreversible tasks. The talk includes a live Python demonstration comparing two versions of the same LLM-powered backend agent. The first runs with permanent credentials, broad access, and no attribution. The second operates under the LAR framework with session-scoped credentials, identity propagation through tool calls, and human approval for sensitive operations. Attendees see the difference between an ungoverned agent and a governed one interacting with the same production-like environment. Key takeaways for attendees: - A clear mental model for governing LLM agents using the LAR framework: Lock, Attribute, Require - Practical patterns for implementing session-scoped credentials for agents in Python to avoid long-lived access tokens - How to use OAuth identity federation to attribute agent tool calls to the initiating user and agent identity - How to introduce human-in-the-loop approval for high-risk agent actions before execution - A practical understanding of the architectural difference between an ungoverned agent and a governed production agent A practical understanding of the architectural difference between an ungoverned agent and a governed production agent I locked my laptop. It is time we built systems that do the same.

Ayo is a London-based backend engineer who has built distributed systems across fintech and SaaS, where the cost of an ungoverned credential is not theoretical. A Software engineer and Educator, she holds an MSc in Software Engineering with Distinction from Kingston University, speaks at DevFest and JSMonthly, and mentors at Codebar and Code Your Future.

Dr David Crepaz-Keay

Dr David Crepaz-Keay

The Mental Health Foundation

As generative AI is increasingly applied to mental health, the risks of harm, misinformation, and inappropriate support grow alongside its potential benefits. This presentation describes the Mental Health Foundation’s approach to building a mental health support bot that is safe, ethical, and effective—placing mental health expertise at the centre of prompt engineering. The work builds on the speaker’s experience developing the original Public Health England Mind Plan tool, now hosted by NHS England, and applies established public mental health principles to large language model design. The talk explores how prompt engineering can be used to define clear role boundaries, prevent diagnostic or therapeutic overreach, respond appropriately to distress, and ensure consistent signposting to human support. Rather than optimising for fluency alone, the Mental Health Foundation’s approach uses prompt constraints, tone guidance, and escalation logic to prioritise safety, trust, and real-world use in sensitive contexts. The session also reflects on collaboration between mental health specialists and technical teams, demonstrating how domain-led prompt design reduces risk while enabling genuinely supportive interactions. This presentation offers a practical case study in building mental health bots that are helpful without being unsafe—and shows why mental health applications require more than generic AI capability.

Dr David Crepaz-Keay, FRSPH, is Head of Research and Applied Learning at the Mental Health Foundation, a 75 year old public mental health NGO. He leads the London based research team and is responsible AI, LLMs and knowledge management systems for the organisation. Dr Crepaz-Keay is a former co-chair and now member of the ethics, policy and position committee of the International Society for Psychiatric Genetics and a fellow of the Royal Society for Public Health. He is an editor of the Journal of Mental Health Training, Education and Practice; the Oxford Handbook of Philosophy and Public Mental Health and the Handbook of Phenomenology, Values and Clinical Decision-Making in Personalised Mental Health Care. He has been a technical advisor for the World Health Organisation, senior mental health advisor to Public Health England, written a mental health module for the Open University, written, spoken and campaigned for improvements in mental health services.

Harrison Kirby

Harrison Kirby

Great Wave AI

Large Language Models are inherently probabilistic systems, yet many of the environments adopting them demand determinism, auditability, and accountability. In these contexts, “mostly correct” is indistinguishable from failure, and reliability cannot be achieved through prompt tuning or model alignment alone. This talk argues that determinism is not a model property but an architectural one: control must be engineered through orchestration, validation layers, policy enforcement, and structured human oversight. It further explores how scaling reliable AI systems is less about adding workflow branches and more about standardizing governed agent primitives, shared contracts, constraints, and evaluation frameworks that allow flexibility within certified boundaries.

Harrison Kirby is CTO and Co-Founder of Great Wave AI and Founding Director of the Centre for GenAIOps. With over 15 years of experience across engineering, operations, and technical leadership, he focuses on building and scaling AI systems that bridge research and real-world deployment. Based in the UK, he works at the intersection of GenAI, platform architecture, and applied innovation.
