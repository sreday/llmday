Dr David Crepaz-Keay

Dr David Crepaz-Keay

The Mental Health Foundation

As generative AI is increasingly applied to mental health, the risks of harm, misinformation, and inappropriate support grow alongside its potential benefits. This presentation describes the Mental Health Foundation’s approach to building a mental health support bot that is safe, ethical, and effective—placing mental health expertise at the centre of prompt engineering. The work builds on the speaker’s experience developing the original Public Health England Mind Plan tool, now hosted by NHS England, and applies established public mental health principles to large language model design. The talk explores how prompt engineering can be used to define clear role boundaries, prevent diagnostic or therapeutic overreach, respond appropriately to distress, and ensure consistent signposting to human support. Rather than optimising for fluency alone, the Mental Health Foundation’s approach uses prompt constraints, tone guidance, and escalation logic to prioritise safety, trust, and real-world use in sensitive contexts. The session also reflects on collaboration between mental health specialists and technical teams, demonstrating how domain-led prompt design reduces risk while enabling genuinely supportive interactions. This presentation offers a practical case study in building mental health bots that are helpful without being unsafe—and shows why mental health applications require more than generic AI capability.

Dr David Crepaz-Keay, FRSPH, is Head of Research and Applied Learning at the Mental Health Foundation, a 75 year old public mental health NGO. He leads the London based research team and is responsible AI, LLMs and knowledge management systems for the organisation. Dr Crepaz-Keay is a former co-chair and now member of the ethics, policy and position committee of the International Society for Psychiatric Genetics and a fellow of the Royal Society for Public Health. He is an editor of the Journal of Mental Health Training, Education and Practice; the Oxford Handbook of Philosophy and Public Mental Health and the Handbook of Phenomenology, Values and Clinical Decision-Making in Personalised Mental Health Care. He has been a technical advisor for the World Health Organisation, senior mental health advisor to Public Health England, written a mental health module for the Open University, written, spoken and campaigned for improvements in mental health services.

Harrison Kirby

Harrison Kirby

Great Wave AI

Large Language Models are inherently probabilistic systems, yet many of the environments adopting them demand determinism, auditability, and accountability. In these contexts, “mostly correct” is indistinguishable from failure, and reliability cannot be achieved through prompt tuning or model alignment alone. This talk argues that determinism is not a model property but an architectural one: control must be engineered through orchestration, validation layers, policy enforcement, and structured human oversight. It further explores how scaling reliable AI systems is less about adding workflow branches and more about standardizing governed agent primitives, shared contracts, constraints, and evaluation frameworks that allow flexibility within certified boundaries.

Harrison Kirby is CTO and Co-Founder of Great Wave AI and Founding Director of the Centre for GenAIOps. With over 15 years of experience across engineering, operations, and technical leadership, he focuses on building and scaling AI systems that bridge research and real-world deployment. Based in the UK, he works at the intersection of GenAI, platform architecture, and applied innovation.

Jake Page

Jake Page

MetalBear

Most people aren't vibe coding an entire app from scratch and shipping it to production without a second thought. What’s more likely to happen is more subtle and potentially riskier. You've got a pre-existing service running in your cluster, it's been reviewed, it has access to your database, your secrets, your internal APIs. Then someone asks an AI assistant to add a feature. The generated code works, great! But, it also hardcodes an API key, or passes raw model output to users, or creates a public ingress to an internal service. And it lands inside a system that already has the keys to everything. The recent Moltbook incident made these blaring security concerns around AI generated code blaringly apparent, a single missing config exposed 1.5 million API keys. At a certain point AI-generated code will make its way into Kubernetes clusters and the security implications are real, are we ready to address them? In this talk we'll break down the specific risks that AI-generated code introduces when it lands in Kubernetes, from pod security and secret management to network exposure and LLM workloads. Then we'll build the fix: open-source security skills you drop into your AI coding assistant so it generates secure defaults automatically. We'll validate those skills against a live cluster using mirrord, no image rebuilds, no redeploys, just fast feedback until the policies actually work. You'll leave with a repo you can use today and an answer to the question every team shipping AI-generated code needs to figure out: if AI writes the code, what reviews it?

Jake Page is a former high-school teacher turned DevOps engineer and now DevRel Engineer. Over the past five years, he has focused on cloud-native developer tooling across packaging, software delivery, FinOps, testing, and debugging, often with a strong Kubernetes angle. Now based in Lisbon, Jake is passionate about learning by doing and frequently speaks at meetups and conferences.

Meryem Arik

Meryem Arik

Doubleword

As models improve, we are starting to build long-running, asynchronous agents such as deep research agents and browser agents that can execute multi-step workflows autonomously. These systems unlock new use cases, but they fail in ways that short-lived agents do not. The longer an agent runs, the more early mistakes compound, and the more token usage grows through extended reasoning, retries, and tool calls. Patterns that work for request-response agents often break down, leading to unreliable behaviour and unpredictable costs. This talk is aimed at use case developers, with secondary relevance for platform engineers. It covers the most common failure modes in async agents and practical design patterns for reducing error compounding and keeping token costs bounded in production.

Meryem Arik is the co-founder and CEO of Doubleword, where she works on large-scale LLM inference and production AI systems. She studied theoretical physics and philosophy at the University of Oxford. Meryem is a frequent conference speaker, including a TEDx speaker and a four-time highly rated speaker at QCon conferences. She was named to the Forbes 30 Under 30 list for her work in AI infrastructure.

Sergey Konstantinov & Alena Panshina

Sergey Konstantinov & Alena Panshina

Bolt EU

“AI agents” and “agentic pipelines” have quickly become this year’s favorite buzzwords. Every product claims to have them. Few teams can clearly explain what actually distinguishes an agent from a prompt chain — or why one agent is fundamentally more capable than another. This talk offers a structured way to think about agent systems using the five levels of AI agents outlined in Google’s Introduction to Agents (Gulli et al.). But instead of treating the framework as gospel, we’ll stress-test it against real implementation scenarios. * What really changes between levels? * Where does orchestration stop and autonomy begin? * At what point does an LLM wrapper become a system? We’ll move through progressively more sophisticated architectures — from deterministic pipelines to systems that plan, reflect, and act with increasing independence. Along the way, we’ll examine the engineering implications: state management, tool integration, evaluation loops, failure modes, and operational complexity.

Susanna Wong

Susanna Wong

Typeform

For decades, we have built deterministic software systems - our architectures, tools, and mental models are optimized for this world. AI software development breaks that mindset. In AI products, software architecture is only the tip of the iceberg. Beneath it lies an evolving data architecture: prompts, retrieval strategies, eval loops, and knowledge graphs that directly shape system behavior over time. Shipping code is only the start of that lifecycle. In this talk, I’ll share how we approach this shift at Typeform as we develop a framework for AI development grounded in agentic RAG. I’ll cover how we evolve both our system architecture and engineering practices—from orchestrating agents using A2A/ MCP patterns, to revamping developer workflows with continuous evals, observability, and tooling that allows teams to safely evolve prompts, graphs, and retrieval strategies. A core focus will be how we design an evolving user knowledge graph, and how RAG acts as shared infra powering all of our AI features. This session is for engineers navigating the transition from deterministic software to AI-native systems, looking for practical patterns to build and operate RAG in production.

Susanna Wong is a Staff Engineer at V7 Labs and a Google Developer Expert with a focus on JavaScript, web technologies, and machine learning. She is a member of the Google Developer Advisory Board and is known for bridging front end engineering with practical ML applications through speaking, teaching, and community work.
